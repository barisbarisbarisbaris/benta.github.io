STARFISH DECAY - Bora Akıncıtürk

September 19 - November 14, 2025

Graphic Design by Berk Çakmakçı

Photographed by Barış Özçetin


Exhibition Text:

THE OBJECT CALLS ON ITS OWNER

Starfish Decay can be read like an atlas of small appropriations: ‘miniature annexations’ that fit into the pocket of everyday life. A tea saucer changes hands as a ‘gift’; a lighter, thought to be lost, is quietly “seized” back by its owner; a ‘Life in the UK’ immigrant adaptation booklet circulates so widely that it is forgotten whose hands it originated from; a T-shirt with a huge Jaws on it is left behind at a friend’s place and finds a new owner; a bandana wrapped in white sand grains, an artefact of a small scale loot dropped from a niche vending machine, fits an illegal shoreline into a jar; a skull ‘executed’ from a fine arts academy workshop then finds its place in someone else's display; a volcanic rock picked up from a restricted area becomes contraband evidence of love for another. All point to the same chronic mode of action: expanding the framework of consent and narrowing the boundaries of responsibility. As ‘finding’ becomes ownership, ‘entrusting’ shifts towards property, and ‘gift’ turns into a mean of domination, our discourse also subtly narrows the field: ‘I took it,’ ‘it remained,’ ‘it's with me.’ The act of big fish eating the little fish begins first on the table we all sit around.

The lost painting is the barometer of this story: the uncertain state of ownership shaped by curatorial practice, institutionalised in a housemate's sniffing of ‘it's better if I don't take it’ and finally reduced to a bin bag. Hence, the distance between the actual object (i.e. the now ‘lost’ painting) and justice becomes starkly apparent. When the artist reproduces the lost painting, they repaint not just an image but also a sense of justice. The severed arm of the starfish first rots, then regenerates as a new arm; the reproduction stands precisely within this duality of time: it holds the loss within it, while also embodying the power to survive. The question of ‘Original or copy?’ bends here into ‘Who owes what to whom and when?’. The possible answers to these questions do not arise from a set of moral prescriptions, but from the eyewitness accounts of the objects in question.

These small scenes of appropriation are pocket-sized models of the large-scale techniques of appropriation we recognise from maps: shifting a boundary stone to the side as a ‘natural extension,’ turning a coastline into a ‘security zone,’ acquiring an asset ‘at a low price.’ The logbook of objects in Starfish Decay can be thought together with the dispossession regimes we have been witnessing in recent history in Eastern Europe and the Middle East: grand history can be heard or reflected on through small actions and objects. Consider the webs of circulation around you, take care of the objects circulating, try to put what you take back in its place, do not neglect to read and reread Cioran. Learning to pick up the stone and put it back, not mistaking the copy for the original but hearing the copy's justified anger... Perhaps Starfish Decay suggests this dictum only: refreshing the ethics of touch where the act of possession has become mouldy. Decay is not always the end, but sometimes the beginning for a shared healing; the severed part holds again when we learn to reach and touch.

Mehmet Ekinci
